How Long Medical Device Financing Takes to Approve — and What Underwriters Actually Check

A specialty lender can approve a $400,000 scanner loan in 30 minutes. A bank needs two to four weeks. An SBA loan needs 45 to 90 days. The difference is not speed — it is what each lender is willing not to know.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026 · 6 min read

The ultrasound machine was already in the building. That was the problem. A cardiology practice in Tampa had ordered a $180,000 system, the vendor had delivered it on a Tuesday, and the financing was still in underwriting on Friday. The vendor wanted payment in 30 days. The practice's bank wanted two years of tax returns, interim financials, a debt schedule, and a personal guarantee — and two to four weeks to decide. The practice called a specialty equipment lender on Monday morning. The approval came back in 30 minutes. The wire landed three days later. The machine was the same. The only thing that changed was the lender's appetite for paperwork, and the price of that appetite was the interest rate.

Medical device financing now comes in three speeds, and the speed tells you what the lender checked. Specialty equipment lenders — the firms that finance nothing but medical and dental equipment — approve most files under $500,000 on the application and the equipment quote alone. No tax returns, no financial statements, no debt schedule. A clean file can be approved in as little as 30 minutes, with funding in one to three days. GFLS, a national medical equipment lender, says complete, organized applications can often receive a credit decision in 24 to 72 hours. Clear Skies Capital funds most equipment deals in 3 to 10 business days. Crestmont Capital approves in hours and funds in 24 to 48 hours. The model works because the equipment is the collateral, the amounts are modest, and the lender knows the resale value of a refurbished ultrasound or a used C-arm the way a bank never will.

Banks are the second speed, and they are slow for a reason that is not incompetence. A traditional bank wants a full financial package: two years of business and personal tax returns, interim financials, and a debt schedule. It typically looks for a personal FICO of 680 or above, with the best pricing above 720, two or more years of operating history, and a debt service coverage ratio of 1.15x to 1.25x on the new debt. Two to four weeks is normal, and that assumes a complete file on day one. The bank is also the lender most likely to want the practice's operating accounts, and the most cautious about used equipment from private sellers, startups, and anything unusual. What the borrower gets in exchange is the lowest rate in the market and a lender that will still be there in year five. What the borrower pays is time — and the risk that the vendor's delivery date passes while the file sits in a queue.

The third speed is the Small Business Administration, and it is the slowest by design. An SBA 7(a) loan takes 45 to 90 days from application to funding, sometimes longer, because two parties review the file: the lender and the SBA itself. The documentation load is the heaviest of the three — business plan, projections, collateral appraisals, site inspections — and the underwriting standards are the most rigorous for medical businesses. The payoff is real: longer terms, lower down payments, and rates that a specialty lender cannot match. For a $2 million imaging suite, the SBA route can be worth the wait. For a single $80,000 equipment purchase, it is usually the wrong tool — the 45-to-90-day timeline and the documentation load are disproportionate, and the rate advantage over a good conventional equipment loan is often modest once fees are counted.

What underwriters actually check depends on which door the borrower walks through, but the list is shorter than most borrowers expect. Credit comes first: specialty lenders commonly want a FICO of 600 or above, with 700-plus for startups, while Clear Skies Capital sets its floor at 660 and wants $250,000 in annual business revenue and two years in business. Cash flow comes second — the practice must cover the new payment with cushion, and the lender will look at bank statements, usually three to six months' worth, to confirm the revenue the tax returns claim. The equipment itself comes third: the lender appraises the machine, checks whether it is new or used, who the vendor is, and what it would fetch at auction if the borrower defaults. A refurbished MRI with a documented service history underwrites cleanly. A used laser bought from a retiring physician's estate does not, because the lender cannot verify its condition or its title.

The red flags that stall a deal are specific, and they are worth knowing before the application goes in. Startups get the hardest look, because there is no operating history to underwrite — the lender is betting on the physician, not the practice. Used equipment from a private seller is the second flag: no vendor warranty, no verified service record, and a resale value the lender has to guess at. The third is undocumented cash. Practices with heavy cash revenue that never appears on a tax return cannot prove the income the lender needs to see, and no amount of explanation fixes a return that says the practice earns less than it does. The fourth is the incomplete file itself. Lenders describe it the same way in every interview: the application that arrives missing the equipment invoice, or the bank statements, or the personal financial statement, sits while complete files move. The delay is rarely the underwriting. It is the paper.

There is a counterargument to the whole speed hierarchy, and it is the one the banks make when nobody is listening. Fast approval is not the same as good financing. A specialty lender that approves in 30 minutes has priced the risk into the rate, and the rate on a fast approval is higher than the rate on a slow one — sometimes by several points, which on a $500,000 loan over five years is tens of thousands of dollars. The borrower who waits two weeks for a bank saves that money. The borrower who waits 90 days for the SBA saves more. Speed is worth paying for only when the delay costs more than the rate: a vendor discount that expires, a tax year that closes, a machine that generates revenue from the day it is installed. The question is never how fast the lender can approve. It is what the delay is worth, in dollars, against what the rate costs.

The practical playbook follows from that arithmetic. If the equipment generates revenue immediately and the vendor's clock is running, take the specialty lender and refinance later if the rate stings — a 24-to-72-hour approval with funding in days beats a perfect rate that arrives after the delivery date. If the purchase is planned and the practice has clean financials, start with the bank two months before the machine is needed, and keep the specialty lender as the fallback. If the deal is large, the practice is established, and the timeline allows it, run the SBA process in parallel and let the rate decide. And whatever the route, send a complete file on day one: application, equipment invoice, six months of bank statements, and the personal financial statement. The fastest approval in the market is the one that never waits for a missing document.

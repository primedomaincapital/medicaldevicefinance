What a Surgical Robot Lease Costs — and What Happens When the Hospital Can't Fill the Schedule

A da Vinci robot costs about $1.6 million to buy and $1,860 in instruments every time it operates. The lease changes who carries that arithmetic — and what happens when the cases don't come.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026 · 6 min read

The robot arrived on a Tuesday in March, and by Friday the chief financial officer of a 220-bed community hospital in Ohio had a problem she had not modelled. The da Vinci Xi in her new operating suite cost roughly $1.6 million — the average selling price Intuitive Surgical reported in the first half of 2026 — and the hospital had signed an operating lease rather than buying it outright. The monthly payment was predictable. The case volume was not. By the end of the first quarter, the robot had done 41 cases. The lease had assumed 90. The gap between those two numbers is where a surgical robot stops being a clinical asset and starts being a financing problem.

Intuitive Surgical does not sell one robot at one price. The company's own filings put the range for a da Vinci system at $600,000 to $3.1 million, depending on model and configuration. The fifth-generation da Vinci 5, cleared by the Food and Drug Administration in March 2024, sits at the top of that range, with more than 2,000 systems installed since launch. The workhorse Xi runs about $1.5 million to $2.5 million. The lower-priced XiR, built for price-sensitive buyers, goes for about $1.2 million, and the single-console X — the entry point for smaller programs — about $500,000. The single-port SP, used mostly in urology, runs $2.0 million to $2.5 million. Those are capital prices. The lease converts them into a monthly number, and the conversion is where the negotiation actually happens.

The lease itself is only the first of three bills. Intuitive reports annual service contracts of $95,000 to $225,000 per system, with the first year usually folded into the purchase price and the meter starting in year two. Then come the instruments: EndoWrist tools that lock out after 10 to 18 uses, at $900 to $3,700 per procedure. Intuitive's own instrument revenue worked out to about $1,860 per procedure in the third quarter of 2026. A program doing 400 cases a year spends roughly $744,000 a year on instruments alone — close to half the price of the machine, every year, for as long as the robot runs. Service contracts add another 10 to 12 percent of the capital price annually. Over five years, the recurring lines dwarf the capital number. That is the point of the business model: in the twelve months to September 2026, Intuitive took in $11.6 billion, and 84 percent of it was recurring.

Leasing has quietly become the default way robots reach hospitals. In the third quarter of 2026, Intuitive placed 546 da Vinci systems, and 53 percent of them went out on operating leases — up from 62 of 537 placements a decade earlier. Of the 291 leased systems that quarter, 160 were usage-based, meaning the hospital pays as it operates rather than a flat monthly rate. Under a usage-based placement, Intuitive installs the system and bills an agreed rate per procedure, provided the hospital holds to minimum quarterly case thresholds. The structure lowers the bar for a hospital that cannot justify a $1.6 million capital purchase, and it raises the bar for Intuitive, which now carries the machine on its own balance sheet. Operating leases typically run three to five years. The hospital gets predictable payments and no capital outlay. The manufacturer gets a customer it would otherwise never have signed.

The arithmetic that decides whether a lease works is brutally simple, and hospitals keep getting it wrong in the same direction. A system idling below one case a day is a financial problem regardless of how well it operates. Take a leased Xi at an all-in cost of roughly $2,600 per case — service, instruments, and the lease payment spread across volume. At 400 cases a year, the per-case cost is manageable against the reimbursement for a robotic prostatectomy or hysterectomy. At 150 cases, the same fixed costs spread across fewer procedures push the per-case number past $4,500, and the program loses money on every case it does. The robot does not care. The lease does not care. The only number that moves is volume, and volume depends on surgeons, referrals, and scheduling — none of which the finance department controls.

That is the risk the usage-based model is designed to share, and it shares it only partway. When a hospital misses its minimum quarterly case threshold, the usage rate rises or a shortfall payment applies, depending on the contract. The manufacturer has protected its downside; the hospital has bought flexibility at the price of a higher per-case rate when volume disappoints. The alternative — a fixed operating lease — is worse in a slow quarter, because the payment is due whether the robot operates or sits dark. The honest comparison is not lease versus purchase. It is fixed lease versus usage-based lease versus purchase, at three different volume scenarios, with the instrument and service lines included in all three. Hospitals that model only the capital price are modelling the smallest number in the deal.

The competitive picture is changing the negotiation, slowly. CMR Surgical's Versius system, a modular multi-arm robot, sells for about $1.0 million to $1.5 million and is frequently placed on usage-based terms, with service bundled into the per-case price rather than billed separately. Medtronic's Hugo system and Stryker's Mako platform — Mako dominates orthopaedic robotics — offer their own lease and placement structures, and Zimmer Biomet's Rosa system competes in the same capital committees. None of them has Intuitive's installed base or its instrument catalog, and none of them has yet forced Intuitive to cut prices. But a hospital that walks into a negotiation with a credible Versius quote gets a better da Vinci lease than a hospital that walks in with nothing. The leverage is the quote, not the threat.

The counterargument is that leasing a robot is the wrong frame entirely, and there is a version of it that deserves a hearing. A hospital that cannot fill a robot's schedule should not have a robot, in any structure. The lease makes it easier to say yes to a machine the program cannot support, and the usage-based model makes it easier still, because the first year's bills look small. The capital purchase, for all its brutality, forces a real decision: the board has to approve $1.6 million, and somebody has to defend the volume forecast in the room. The lease lets the same decision slide through as an operating expense, approved at a lower threshold, with the volume risk discovered a year later. The robot vendors know this. It is why 53 percent of placements are leases now, and why the share keeps rising.

What a hospital should do is narrower and more boring than the sales pitch. Model the lease at the volume the program will actually do in year one, not the volume the surgeons promise. Price the instruments at $1,860 a case and the service at the top of the $95,000-to-$225,000 range, because both numbers rise with case mix. Get the minimum quarterly threshold in writing, with the shortfall price stated as a dollar figure, not a formula. And put a decision date in the contract — twelve months in, with the volume actuals on the table — at which the hospital can return the system, convert to a purchase, or renegotiate. The robot is a fine machine. The lease is a bet on volume. Bet on the volume you have, not the volume you hope for.

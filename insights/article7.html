The Refurbished Scanner: What Used Imaging Equipment Costs — and What It Risks

A refurbished 1.5T MRI costs $475,000 to $975,000. The same machine new costs $1.2 million to $2 million. The $700,000 gap is real — and so are the three things it does not buy.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026 · 6 min read

The magnet arrived on a flatbed in February, and the imaging center in Fort Myers had paid $640,000 for it — a refurbished GE Optima MR450w, delivered, installed, and warrantied. The same machine new would have cost roughly $1.5 million. The center's owner did the arithmetic every buyer does: the refurbished scanner cost about 40 percent of new, it came with a warranty, and it would scan patients the same week it was installed. Eighteen months later the scanner has done 4,200 studies and the only surprise was the service contract, which cost more than the quote had suggested. The refurbished market runs on exactly that trade. The discount is real. The risks are specific. The buyers who do well are the ones who price the risks before they sign.

The price ladder for refurbished MRI is set by field strength first and condition second. In 2026, a refurbished open or low-field system costs $300,000 to $525,000, a refurbished 1.5T costs $475,000 to $975,000, and a refurbished 3T costs $1,000,000 to $1,900,000 — delivered, installed, and warrantied, according to Medical Outfitters' 2026 price guide. The new equivalents run $600,000 to $950,000, $1,200,000 to $2,000,000, and $2,100,000 to $3,700,000. Equipment-only prices on the secondary market run lower still: Amber Diagnostics' 2026 pre-owned pricing starts around $100,000 to $250,000 for older foundation-level systems. The gap between equipment-only and all-in is the whole story of the refurbished market. A $100,000 magnet is cheap. Rigging it into a building, ramping it up, installing the coils and software, and warrantying the result is where the other $400,000 goes.

Model-level prices tell the same story with more precision. A refurbished GE Signa HDxt 1.5T runs $475,000 to $675,000; the wide-bore Optima MR450w, $600,000 to $900,000; the Discovery MR750w, $1,100,000 to $1,750,000. Siemens' Espree runs $475,000 to $700,000, the Aera $650,000 to $975,000, and the Skyra above $1,000,000. Philips' Ingenia 1.5T sits across the $475,000-to-$975,000 band, and the Ingenia 3.0T runs $1,100,000 to $1,900,000. Fujifilm's OASIS Velocity, the 1.2T open system that competes with closed 1.5T machines on image quality, costs $1,225,000 to $1,775,000 certified pre-owned. The pattern is consistent: the discount against new runs 40 to 60 percent on most modalities, and the newest generation of any line commands the top of its band. A buyer who wants the latest gradients and the widest bore pays for them, refurbished or not.

What the discount does not buy is the part buyers discover later, and it comes in three forms. The first is the service horizon. A new system comes with a manufacturer warranty and a service contract priced for a machine with its full life ahead of it. A refurbished system comes with a warranty from the refurbisher — typically one year — and then a third-party service contract. Current third-party full-service pricing runs about $70,000 to $134,000 a year for GE systems, $86,000 to $125,000 for Philips, and $74,000 to $120,000 for Siemens. Over five years, service adds $350,000 to $670,000 to the cost of the machine. The second is capability. A refurbished system is a previous generation: older gradients, older software, and a coil package that may not include the sequences a referring physician now expects. The third is parts. Older magnets consume helium, and the cold head — not the helium — becomes the service item on newer zero-boil-off systems like Siemens' Tim 4G magnets. A buyer who does not check the magnet generation is buying a service bill, not a scanner.

The manufacturers have noticed the size of the secondary market, and their responses split it in two. GE HealthCare, Siemens Healthineers, and Philips all run certified refurbishment programs of their own, and the premium for a manufacturer-refurbished system over an independent one is the warranty and the parts pipeline. Medical Outfitters, an independent refurbisher, rebuilds GE, Siemens, Philips, Canon, and Hitachi systems through its MO Select program, is ISO 13485 certified, and repairs MRI coils in-house — the kind of capability that separates a real refurbisher from a broker with a warehouse. Fujifilm, which took over the Hitachi lines, is the strongest name in open MRI and sells new as an authorized dealer. Canon, formerly Toshiba, has a smaller US installed base and a thinner independent service market in some regions. The buyer's question is not which brand is best. It is who services the machine in the buyer's city, and what happens when a coil fails on a Friday.

The service contract is where the refurbished deal is actually priced, and it is the line buyers negotiate least. A third-party contract that covers parts, labor, and the cold head at $70,000 to $134,000 a year for a GE system looks expensive until the alternative is priced: a single gradient amplifier replacement can cost $40,000 to $80,000, a coil $15,000 to $30,000, and a magnet quench — rare, but real — six figures plus weeks of downtime. The contract converts those spikes into a flat number the practice can budget. The negotiation that matters is not the annual price but the response time, the uptime guarantee, and whether loaner coils are included. A contract that promises a 48-hour response and delivers a coil in six days is worth less than a cheaper contract from a servicer with a parts depot in the same state. Ask where the parts live before asking what the contract costs.

The counterargument is that refurbished imaging is a false economy, and it is worth stating plainly. A refurbished scanner is older technology with a shorter remaining life, a service contract that costs more per year than a new machine's, and a resale value that falls faster. A hospital that buys new gets the latest gradients, the full warranty, the manufacturer service network, and a machine that will still be current in seven years. The refurbished buyer saves $700,000 on day one and spends a meaningful share of it back on service, downtime, and the earlier replacement. The arithmetic only works if the buyer actually banks the savings — and many buyers spend them on the installation, the site work, and the coils they forgot to budget. The discount is real. So is the bill that follows it.

What separates the buyers who win from the buyers who regret it is a short list of checks, and none of them is exotic. Confirm the software level and the coil list in writing before signing, because the quote that omits a coil is a quote for a different machine. Ask who holds the service contract after the warranty year, and what the annual price is — not the first-year price, the fifth-year price. Check the magnet: a zero-boil-off system changes the service economics completely. Verify the refurbisher's certification and ask for the test protocol the system passed, not a marketing claim. And price the installation separately from the machine, because rigging, shielding, and site work routinely add six figures that the equipment price never mentions. A refurbished scanner at $640,000 that scans 4,200 patients in eighteen months is a good purchase. The same scanner bought without those checks is a $640,000 lesson.

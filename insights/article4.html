The Tariff on the Scanner

Tariffs added hundreds of millions to the cost of imaging equipment in 2025. The manufacturers absorbed most of it — and hospitals are about to find out what that means for the next scanner they finance.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 9, 2026 · 7 min read

On September 26, 2025, a short notice appeared in the Federal Register. The Commerce Department had opened an investigation into imports of personal protective equipment, medical consumables, and medical equipment — including the CT scanners, MRI machines, and X-ray systems that fill American hospitals. The notice set no tariff. It asked whether one was needed, on national-security grounds. In a hospital finance office somewhere, a chief financial officer read it and did the arithmetic that matters: the scanner she had budgeted at $2.1 million might not cost $2.1 million by the time the purchase order was signed.

By the spring of 2026, the arithmetic had a price tag. GE HealthCare told investors its gross tariff exposure for 2025 was about $500 million, roughly $375 million of it tied to China. Siemens Healthineers forecast €400 million of tariff cost for its fiscal 2026, after €200 million in fiscal 2025. Stryker guided to about $400 million for 2026. These were not the numbers that made the news. The ones that did were smaller and sharper: a 100% tariff on gloves and syringes from January 1, 2026, under the Section 301 China measures; steel, aluminum, and copper tariffs as high as 50%; and an April 2026 metals proclamation that began assessing duties on the full customs value of derivative products rather than on their metal content alone. A device with 8% steel content by value that once paid duty on that 8% could now pay on 100% of its value. The 15% rate under the U.S.–EU framework applied to many EU-sourced medical technology exports, a cost Siemens flagged directly in its guidance.

The reason tariffs bite imaging harder than most equipment is that the supply chain cannot be moved. Superconducting MRI magnets are still wound in Oxford, England, because that is where the magnet line and the people who run it sit. The cyclotrons that make PET doses — $2 million to $4 million accelerators — come from a small set of firms in Belgium, Japan, and Canada. The isotope behind most nuclear-medicine scans, technetium-99m, is made from molybdenum-99 that the United States still imports from a handful of reactors in Europe, South Africa, and Australia. Taxing it on arrival does not conjure a domestic reactor. And even where a domestic supplier exists, changing a single component vendor can require a full FDA recertification — a process measured in months and millions. The industry group AdvaMed puts the scale of what is at stake in plain numbers: American-made products hold about 70% of the domestic medical technology market by value, supporting about three million direct and indirect jobs and generating $250 billion a year from more than 16,000 facilities in all 50 states, with about $80 billion exported in 2024.

The bill lands on hospitals that cannot pass it on. Kaufman Hall reported that 43% of American hospitals were operating at a financial loss as of August 2026, up from roughly 33% before the pandemic, and national operating margins fell 5% year over year through the first five months of the year. Vizient projects capital and imaging equipment inflation of 3.21% for 2027 — imaging equipment alone at 2.96%. Coupa's business-spend data showed healthcare purchasing commitments falling 2.8% between February and June 2026, the only major sector to shrink. A Premier survey found 82% of healthcare executives expected tariffs to raise their costs by 15% within six months. And reimbursement, which is where a hospital recovers the cost of a scan, is moving the other way: the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services' proposed 2027 outpatient rule would extend site-neutral payment to imaging without contrast — most X-rays and MRIs in off-campus departments — cutting Part B spending by about $260 million in the first year, alongside a $4.55 billion cut to 340B drug payments. A radiology practice operates on fixed reimbursement schedules. When the machine gets more expensive and the payment for using it gets smaller, the gap has to come from somewhere.

The radiology world pushed back in the one forum the Commerce Department opened. The American College of Radiology and AdvaMed filed comments ahead of the October 17 deadline, joined by RadNet, the Radiology Business Management Association, and the Radiological Society of North America — which represents more than 52,000 imaging professionals across 150 countries. RSNA chair Jeffrey S. Klein warned that supply-chain disruption could raise costs and delay care, with a direct effect on health outcomes. RBMA president Jamie Dyer wrote that providers were already facing financial hardship, and that more than 700 rural hospitals were at risk of closure — adding tariff costs could force many to shutter or cut services. The Japanese imaging industry association, JIRA, whose members include Fujifilm, welcomed a U.S.–Japan agreement settling on a simple 15% tariff, then warned that further measures could mean significant price increases and reduced investment in America. The argument from every quarter was the same: the machines are not a luxury import. They are the diagnostic infrastructure, and taxing them taxes the diagnosis.

Here is the part of the story that did not make the headlines. The big manufacturers mostly absorbed the cost instead of passing it through. GE HealthCare's actual 2025 tariff bill came in at $245 million, less than half its initial gross estimate, and its expected 2026 impact fell below $25 million once refunds were counted. In the second quarter of 2026, its operating cash flow included a $107 million tariff refund; the third quarter brought another $55 million. The company raised its full-year guidance in October — revenue up 7.2% in the third quarter, adjusted earnings per share now $4.95 to $5.05 — with chief executive Peter Arduini crediting volume, price, and cost actions. Siemens Healthineers reported a net tariff benefit in its second fiscal quarter and raised its earnings guidance to €2.35 to €2.45 a share. Part of the relief came from the courts: the Supreme Court blocked some of the tariff authorities in February 2026, and manufacturers began applying for refunds through the Customs and Border Protection portal. The tariff bill of 2025 turned out to be, for the largest manufacturers, a cost they could carry.

That is the counterargument, and it has teeth. Gross margins in the high 60s to high 70s, pricing power, and balance sheets that can carry a multi-hundred-million-dollar cost while mitigation catches up — the big three had all three. But the absorption came from somewhere, and the likeliest somewhere is the research budget: the same dollars that fund the next generation of scanners. Smaller manufacturers, without that cushion, had no such choice. And the investigation that started all of this is still open. The Section 232 inquiry into medical equipment imports passed its 270-day report deadline in late May or June 2026 with no Commerce report published and no presidential proclamation issued — as of August 2026 the Bureau of Industry and Security still listed it as ongoing. The tariff that would matter most for imaging may not have arrived yet. For a hospital weighing a purchase, that uncertainty is itself a cost: MonitorDaily's 2026 market update notes that margin-tight hospitals now favor structures aligned to cash-flow timing — deferrals and step-ups — and that tariff exposure argues for refresh options that hedge parts pricing and delivery risk.

The lesson for anyone financing a scanner is that the price of the machine is now partly a policy variable. A ten-year lease signed on the assumption that a $2.1 million MRI costs $2.1 million is a bet that trade policy stays still for a decade — and 2025 and 2026 proved it does not. The structures that make sense now are the ones that move the risk to the party best placed to carry it: shorter terms with refresh options, so a hospital is not locked into yesterday's price; pay-per-use contracts, where the manufacturer absorbs the parts cost because it controls the supply chain; service-wrapped leases that separate the machine from the software and the maintenance. The hospital that finances its next scanner is not just underwriting its own credit. It is underwriting trade policy. The lenders and lessors who price that honestly will write the next decade of imaging finance. The ones who price the machine as if its cost were fixed will find out otherwise, one revised quote at a time.

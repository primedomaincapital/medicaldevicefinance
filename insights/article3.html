The Practices the Banks Will Not Touch

A dental practice buying a $90,000 imaging unit is too small for a bank and too slow for a payday lender. Specialty healthcare lenders live in that gap.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 8, 2026 · 7 min read

Consider a three-dentist practice outside Tampa — a composite of the kind of borrower specialty lenders describe, not a single named case. Last spring the practice decided to replace its panoramic X-ray unit with a 3D cone-beam system. The quote was $90,000, installed. The owner called her bank, where she has held a business account for eleven years. The loan officer was polite, thorough, and slow: six weeks of statements, tax returns, and committee review, and then a no. The amount was too small to justify the underwriting, the collateral too specialized to resell, and her student loans — paid down, but still on the books — made the credit model flinch. She had a profitable practice, a waiting list of patients, and no way to buy the machine that would let her treat them.

She is the customer the specialty healthcare lenders were built for. BHG Financial began in 2001 as Bankers Healthcare Group, founded on the observation that doctors, dentists, and veterinarians look risky to a conventional credit model — high income, high student debt, lumpy reimbursement cycles — and are, in practice, among the most reliable borrowers in the country. The company has since funded more than $28 billion across 257,000-plus customers, with business loans up to $500,000 and terms up to 12 years, and approval in as little as 24 hours with funding in three to five days. That is the whole pitch: a lender that underwrites the profession, not the spreadsheet.

National Business Capital works the same gap from the other end. A direct lender with 18 years in the market and more than $3 billion deployed, it offers medical equipment financing from $250,000 to $15 million, for businesses with at least one year in operation, $500,000 in annual revenue, and a FICO score of 600 or above. The thresholds matter. A bank's healthcare division wants a hospital system. National Business Capital wants the imaging center, the ambulatory surgical center, the multi-physician group — the middle of the market, where the equipment is expensive enough to need financing and the borrower is too small to command a bank's attention. Wells Fargo Practice Finance, the largest bank in this space, offers up to 100% financing for startups, acquisitions, equipment, and commercial real estate — and even that program exists because the bank recognized that healthcare practices needed a dedicated desk, not a general commercial loan officer.

The gap exists because of what the equipment costs at the bottom of the market. A radiology practice's X-ray and ultrasound systems run $20,000 to $200,000; a refurbished fixed digital X-ray room runs $60,000 to $135,000; a refurbished 64-slice CT runs $175,000 to $375,000. These are not numbers that move a bank's quarterly earnings. They are numbers that decide whether a small practice can offer a service at all. A clinic that cannot finance a $90,000 imaging unit does not buy a cheaper one. It refers the patient elsewhere, and the revenue follows the referral. The financing decision is a clinical decision, made by a lender who will never meet the patient.

The economics of the gap explain why the specialty lenders charge what they charge. BHG typically requires a credit score of 700 or above, and its rates run higher than a bank's — the price of speed, of unsecured lending, and of underwriting a borrower the bank declined. National Business Capital's advisor-guided model takes two to eight weeks, slower than BHG's one to five days, because the amounts are larger and the underwriting heavier. Credibly and the other alternative lenders fill the space below them, for borrowers who cannot clear even those bars. Each tier of the market is a trade: speed against price, size against scrutiny, certainty against cost.

The counterargument is that this market exists because the banks are right. A lender that approves a loan in 24 hours is a lender that has not looked very hard, and the history of fast, expensive credit to small businesses is not a happy one. The specialty lenders' own numbers — $28 billion funded, 257,000 customers — are a measure of volume, not of outcomes, and the default rates that would settle the question are not published. A practice that borrows at a premium to buy equipment it cannot quite afford is taking on the same risk the bank declined, at a higher price. The gap is real. Whether filling it at these rates helps the practice or merely postpones its failure is a question the lenders' marketing does not answer.

What happens next is consolidation of the gap itself. The global medical equipment financing market is growing at 7% to 8.5% a year by every published estimate, and the fastest growth is in the segments the specialty lenders serve: patient monitoring equipment at 9.65% a year, refurbished equipment at 12.48%, pay-per-use subscriptions at 12.77%. As more of the market moves to smaller, cheaper, and refurbished machines — the machines a single practice can actually buy — the lenders who understand a dentist's cash flow will matter more than the banks who understand a hospital system's bond rating. The banks will keep the $3 million rooms. The practices will be financed by the people who answer the phone.
